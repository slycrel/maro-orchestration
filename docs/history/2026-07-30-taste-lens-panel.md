## The harvest (cross-lens structure)

1. **Unanimous: reconstruct before building.** All five independently
   ratified reconstruct-don't-build, each contributing an instrument:
   trace viewer / frame capture (engine), fork practice arena with
   contrast sets (expertise), shot list with Shadow-Blocking-Coverage
   columns (DP), loop map with delay coding (systems), fork log +
   bandit baseline (ML). These are five column-families of ONE
   fork-replay readout — the camera readout's spec just got written by
   committee.
2. **Three-lens consensus hit on our nicest conclusion.** "Judgement
   makes manufactured practice trustworthy" was attacked as
   necessary-but-not-sufficient from three directions: Goodharted/
   proxy-shaped judgement criteria (systems), simulator overfit +
   verifier-quirk training (ML), and the missing coach function
   (expertise). The practice-arena honesty contract now has three
   named threats to answer, not zero.
3. **Two-lens consensus on n<10.** Optimistic unless the update target
   is narrow — with the same salvage from both: small, narrow,
   consequence-attributed corrections ("library of scars" ≈ rules,
   rubrics, routing heuristics). Notably this is exactly the
   seed-level-edit claim from the conversation, now with its ceiling
   stated.
4. **Two lenses named what the card omits: time.** Temporal coherence
   across steps (engine) and blocking/coverage — staging and
   capture-for-the-edit (DP). The card as drafted is a single-frame
   instrument; taste is sequential.
5. **The vantage essence got corrected.** The Battlefield Earth
   "secret-revealing angle" is a semi-truth by its author's own label;
   the DP completed it: there is no objective shot, only the useful
   partial truth for this moment — a framing is a *chosen lie* and the
   tradeoff (what this view sacrifices) should be on the ledger with
   the choice.

## Pattern verdict (the recursive test)

**PASS.** Five lenses produced five distinct missing-concepts with
near-zero overlap (interest management / mental representations /
coverage / policy resistance / contextual bandits) and a discriminating
3-1-1 split on strongest-idea. The panel surfaced contrast against
*both* participants — corrected Jeremy's vantage essence AND the shared
judgement-makes-practice-trustworthy conclusion — which is what
"contrasting information falls out" was asking for. Cost: five
one-shot calls, ~1 minute wall-clock each. This is the vantage-diverse
cousin of the 2026-07-22 lens_ablation finding (costume 0.12 vs
evidence 0.20 overlap): vantage diversity in the *charge* produced
non-overlapping harvests even at one-shot depth. Caveats, honestly:
n=1 conversation, no control arm (no 5-identical-persona baseline this
time), and lens outputs are perspectives, not verified findings —
every borrowed concept gets verified against our actual machinery
before it drives a build.

## Appendix — full lens outputs (verbatim)

### engine-architect

1. **Strongest Idea**

The strongest idea is the inversion: the LLM is the player, the harness is the engine.

That is not just a metaphor. It gives you the right ownership boundary. The model does not need the whole world state; it needs the currently renderable slice of world state that preserves agency, orientation, and intent. In engine terms, the context assembler is doing scene submission. It decides what enters the frame, at what resolution, with what occlusion, and what stale state gets culled.

That makes "taste" less mystical. Taste is not primarily memory, ranking, or training. It is frame construction under budget. What do you draw this frame? What do you leave out? What stale artifact gets represented as grey fog instead of live geometry? What affordance do you expose so the player takes the right next action?

This also explains the failure modes better than the learning language does. A confused LLM is often not a weak player. It is a player dropped into a broken level: bad spawn point, wrong mission objective, inconsistent save state, stale HUD, or lore from another map leaking into the scene.

2. **What They Missed**

They are underweighting frame time.

The conversation talks about LOD, camera, immersion, reps, and lenses, but not enough about the per-frame budget discipline that makes those systems work. A game engine does not ask "what is the best possible scene?" It asks: what can I submit this frame at 16.6 ms, with stable latency, without blowing memory, while preserving the illusion?

For maro, every fork has equivalent budgets: tokens, wall time, verifier cost, tool latency, context pollution, and operator attention. Taste should be modeled as a scheduler under hard budgets, not just a camera operator. The camera is downstream of scheduling.

They also missed temporal coherence. Immersion breaks are rarely caused by one bad frame. They are caused by inconsistent deltas across frames: the quest marker moves without explanation, the NPC forgets what just happened, the player's inventory silently changes. In maro terms, the danger is not merely bad prompt framing. It is unstable continuity between steps. A prompt can be locally excellent and globally destructive if it invalidates the player's mental model.

The "settings card" risks becoming a debug UI mistaken for an engine system. Subject / zoom / lens / light / focus / exposure is useful if it produces better frame submissions. But if every fork computes a decorative camera manifest that nobody measures against cost and outcome, it becomes unpaid abstraction.

The primitives ledger has the same risk. Seven primitives is fine. Seven primitives plus unconstrained pairwise interactions becomes a soft architecture unless each primitive owns a clear runtime responsibility, cost envelope, and failure signature.

3. **Missing Concept**

They need **interest management**.

In multiplayer engines, interest management decides which entities, updates, and state changes each client receives. You do not replicate the whole world. You replicate the subset relevant to that player's position, velocity, line of sight, team, predicted future interactions, and bandwidth budget.

That is almost exactly maro's context problem.

Retrieval is not enough. Retrieval asks "what matches?" Interest management asks "what must this player know now to act correctly, what can be approximated, what must be suppressed, and what must be streamed later if the player moves that way?"

This gives sharper machinery than "camera" alone:

- Relevance radius: what state is near the current fork?
- Threat radius: what omitted state could cause catastrophic wrong action?
- Prediction horizon: what will likely matter in the next 1-3 steps?
- Replication priority: what must be rendered live vs summarized vs hidden?
- Staleness policy: when does grey fog require a re-scout?
- Anti-entropy: how do you repair divergence between durable world state and the player's current context?

That last one matters. Distributed game state always drifts. Good engines have reconciliation. Maro needs the same thing for prompt context.

4. **What I Would Do Next**

I would build a trace viewer before building new taste machinery.

For each fork, render a "frame capture":

- Submitted context chunks, with byte/token cost.
- Omitted candidate chunks above some relevance threshold.
- Staleness age of each included fact.
- Prompt objective, done-means, verifier path.
- Prior step state and changed state.
- Outcome verdict and failure class.
- Whether the LLM exhibited disorientation: repeated ask, contradicted prior state, role confusion, irrelevant plan, stale assumption.

Then I would replay 50-100 historical forks and classify failures as engine failures or player failures.

Engine failures: wrong state rendered, stale state trusted, missing objective, bad LOD, cross-run contamination, too much irrelevant context, no visible affordance.

Player failures: model had the right state and still reasoned badly.

Only after that would I build the camera card, and only for axes that explain failures in the replay. The first shippable system should probably be an interest-management policy for context assembly: live state, grey state, threat state, and suppressed state, all logged per fork. That gives taste a frame budget, a profiler, and measurable immersion breaks.

### expertise-researcher

1. **Strongest Idea**

The strongest idea is not "taste as camera work." It is the claim that judgement can make manufactured practice trustworthy.

In Ericsson terms, this is the first part that stops being metaphor and becomes an acquisition mechanism. Deliberate practice requires tasks just beyond current ability, rapid feedback, error correction, and repeatable attempts against a known standard. Most AI "learning from experience" talk skips the feedback-quality problem. This conversation at least sees it: reps are only useful if the arena tells the truth.

That matters because "taste" is otherwise too vague. If the system can generate variants of failed fork decisions, try alternative framings, and receive reliable judgement about downstream effects, then taste becomes trainable as a discrimination skill: "In situations like this, this framing reveals the decisive structure; that one creates noise." That is much closer to how chess players, radiologists, and musicians acquire intuition than accumulating lessons in a memory store.

2. **What They Got Wrong Or Missed**

They are overestimating how much can be gained from a few failures. Jeremy's `n < 10` instinct is useful as ambition, but expertise research would treat it with suspicion. A handful of reps can correct a local misconception. It does not usually build robust intuition unless the task class is already tightly bounded and the learner already has strong mental representations.

They also risk confusing evocative framing with trainable representation. "Camera angle," "lens," "immersion," and "player" may be good design language, but expertise is not built from metaphors. It is built from increasingly precise perceptual discriminations: what cues matter, what cues are noise, what patterns predict failure, what action is available next. If the camera vocabulary does not become an observable coding scheme, it will feel profound while remaining unmeasurable.

The population-priors idea is especially dangerous. Human public behavior data may provide broad priors, but transfer usually fails when surface similarity hides different feedback structure. Internet users' browsing patterns do not automatically teach an orchestration system when to build a render-to-LLM browser. They may teach what humans commonly attempt, not what an expert orchestrator should attempt.

They also underweight coaching. In deliberate practice, the learner does not merely repeat tasks and receive verdicts. A coach selects tasks, isolates weaknesses, prevents compensatory habits, and raises difficulty at the right moment. If maro manufactures its own reps, then the "coach" function must be explicit. Otherwise the system will practice what is easy to simulate, not what most improves taste.

3. **Missing Concept**

The missing concept is **mental representations**.

Experts do not just "have taste." They encode domain situations differently. A chess master sees attacking motifs and weak squares; a novice sees pieces. A surgeon sees tissue planes and risk fields; a novice sees anatomy labels. The expert's intuition is fast because the representation already contains action-relevant structure.

For maro, the core question should be: what internal representation of a fork allows better taste? The seven primitives may be ingredients, but they are not yet a mental representation. A useful representation would let the system look at a step boundary and perceive, for example: "underspecified objective," "stale world model," "premature construction," "verification unavailable," "wrong altitude," "context contamination risk," or "cheap vantage shift available."

That representation is what practice should train. Not prompts in general. Not taste in general. The perceptual encoding of orchestration situations.

4. **What I Would Do Next**

I would build a small deliberate-practice arena around fork decisions only.

First, collect 50-100 historical fork points: moments where the system chose what to attempt next or how to frame a worker prompt. For each, reconstruct the local state, hide the actual choice, and ask multiple agents or prompt variants to choose the next move.

Second, create an error taxonomy grounded in observable consequences: wrong altitude, wrong lens, missing verification path, stale-context trust, premature build, failure to re-scout, over-broad prompt, role confusion, irrelevant memory injection.

Third, score choices before looking at final task success. Taste needs proximal feedback. "Did the whole run succeed?" is too delayed and confounded. Better: did this fork reduce uncertainty, preserve option value, expose the right constraint, or create verifiable progress?

Fourth, manufacture contrast sets. Take one failed fork and vary only one feature: same task with fresh vs stale context, same task with build vs observe available, same task with narrow vs broad done-means. This is how you train discrimination.

Only after that would I formalize the camera card. Reconstruct first, label failures, test whether the labels predict better choices, then turn the surviving labels into machinery.

### cinematographer

1. **Strongest Idea**

The strongest idea is the split between moving the camera and building a lens.

That is real craft. On set, most "taste" is not mystical. It is knowing whether the shot is wrong because you are standing in the wrong place, using the wrong focal length, lighting the wrong plane, or trying to photograph something the current rig cannot reveal. Those are different decisions with different costs.

The useful part for maro is this: a good DP does not improve the image by adding more description. I improve it by changing the viewing condition. Move six feet left. Drop the lens height. Go longer and compress the background. Flag the spill. Turn the actor instead of the camera. Suddenly the same reality yields a different truth.

That maps cleanly to prompt orchestration. Taste is not only "pick the next task." It is: what viewing condition will make the task legible to the model? Sometimes that is cheap reframing. Sometimes it is scouting. Sometimes it is a new instrument. A render-to-LLM browser is not a better prompt; it is a lens purchase.

2. **What They Missed**

They are over-romanticizing "the right angle." There is rarely one magic vantage. There is a chosen lie.

Every camera position reveals and conceals. A wide lens gives geography but distorts faces. A long lens finds emotional isolation but destroys spatial truth. High angle clarifies blocking but weakens power. Low angle gives dominance but hides floor marks, eyelines, and sometimes the actual action. Lighting is the same: when I shape attention, I am also creating ignorance.

So the system needs to treat framing as a tradeoff ledger, not a revelation machine. "This view makes X visible by sacrificing Y." Without that, camera language will seduce them into thinking taste means finding the objective shot. It does not. It means choosing the useful partial truth for this moment in the sequence.

They also underweight blocking. Camera people who only talk lenses are amateurs. The frame is half camera, half movement. If the actor crosses too early, if the business happens upstage, if the important hand is hidden behind a shoulder, no lens saves you. In maro terms: do not only reframe the prompt around the task. Re-block the work. Change ordering, dependency, role, handoff, and who is "in the scene" before asking whether the model saw it correctly.

3. **Missing Concept**

They need **coverage**.

Coverage is the discipline of making sure the scene can survive the edit. Master, overs, singles, inserts, reactions, cutaways, maybe a clean plate. Not because every shot is beautiful, but because the editor needs handles. Taste on set includes knowing what must be captured now because it cannot be invented later.

For maro, coverage means deliberate alternate captures of the same problem: a wide/master view for map coherence, a tight insert for the uncertain mechanism, a reaction shot from verification, a cutaway from precedent or prior runs. The point is not five personas as decoration. The point is editorial optionality. Can the later judgement/editing phase assemble a coherent result from the material produced?

4. **What I'd Do Next**

I would build a "shot list" for failed runs, not a taste card.

For each meaningful fork, record:

- **Master:** what is the whole scene?
- **Subject:** what must the audience/model actually understand?
- **Camera position:** from whose vantage is this being viewed?
- **Lens:** what abstraction/tool/persona is doing the seeing?
- **Light:** what context is intentionally revealed?
- **Shadow:** what context is intentionally withheld?
- **Blocking:** what work order or interaction pattern is being staged?
- **Coverage:** what alternate angle must be captured before moving on?
- **Edit risk:** what will be impossible to verify or reconstruct later?

Then run this against past failures. I would not ask "did the metaphor fit?" I would ask: where did we lack coverage, where was the subject obscured, where did blocking make the action unreadable, and where did we buy a new lens when a camera move would have done?

That will separate useful cinematography from camera-flavored poetry very quickly.

### systems-thinker

1. **Strongest Idea**

The strongest idea is not "taste as camera work." It is that taste is being relocated from an internal property of the LLM to the loop structure around it.

That matters. If the LLM is the player and the harness is the game engine, then taste is no longer treated as a mysterious stock hidden inside the model. It becomes a set of flows into decision moments: what context is rendered, what alternatives are made visible, what constraints are salient, what feedback returns, and how quickly the next attempt is shaped by that feedback.

That is a much better systems move than trying to "teach taste" directly. They are shifting leverage from parameter tuning to information structure. In Meadows terms, they are moving above constants and weights toward changing the structure of information flows: who sees what, when, at what resolution, with what memory, and under what consequences.

The "purpose of a system is what it does" test also favors this idea. A system that merely stores lessons does not necessarily develop taste. It develops archives. A system that repeatedly changes what the actor can perceive at the fork may actually develop taste-like behavior, because its observable purpose becomes better framing of action before action.

2. **What They Missed**

They are still too enchanted by primitives.

A ledger of seven primitives is useful vocabulary, but systems behavior will not come from the primitives. It will come from dominance shifts among feedback loops. Which loop wins when they conflict? Does negative-space reduction suppress exploration too early? Does lens-building become an attractive sink for effort? Do population priors overpower local evidence? Do manufactured reps train to the arena instead of the world?

They note "interactions are the machinery," but they have not yet drawn the loop structure. That is the miss.

The dangerous loop is this:

Taste proposes a framing → judgement verifies the outcome → successful framings are reinforced → future forks see similar framings sooner.

That is a reinforcing loop. It can generate expertise, but it can also generate lock-in, monoculture, premature abstraction, and Goodharted taste. Judgement does not automatically make practice trustworthy. Judgement makes the feedback leg stronger. If the judgement criteria are narrow, delayed, or proxy-shaped, the whole system will become more confidently wrong.

They also underweight delays. Taste failures often do not show up at the step boundary. A framing can look successful locally while degrading future maneuverability, narrowing context, or causing later integration debt. If judgement is mostly immediate outcome verification, it will reward short-loop wins and miss long-loop harm.

3. **Missing Concept**

They need **policy resistance** on the table.

In complex systems, interventions often get absorbed, counteracted, or inverted by the system's existing feedback structure. Here, every taste mechanism will induce compensating behavior elsewhere.

Examples:

A stronger framing card may make prompts cleaner but reduce surprise.

A render-to-LLM browser may improve web use but create dependency on one representational lens.

Manufactured reps may improve benchmarked recovery while reducing sensitivity to genuinely novel situations.

Population priors may import human compression, but also human blind spots and majority-path bias.

Policy resistance asks: "When we pull this leverage point, what loop pushes back?" That question is currently more important than adding another primitive.

4. **What I Would Do Next**

I would build a loop map before building more mechanism.

Concretely, I would take 20 to 40 historical runs and code each fork for four things: perceived state, rendered context, attempted action, feedback returned. Then I would mark delay length: immediate, same-run, later-run, or never observed.

From that, I would identify the active loops:

Reinforcing loops that amplify a framing.

Balancing loops that correct bad framing.

Missing loops where bad taste has no consequence.

Delayed loops where the consequence arrives too late to train the chooser.

Then I would run one small intervention: manufactured reps only on failures where the feedback loop is short, honest, and externally checkable. No broad taste system yet. The goal would be to measure whether the loop improves future fork framing, not merely whether the replay succeeds.

The key metric should not be "did the rep pass?" It should be: "Did the next real run change its perception-action structure in a way that avoided the prior failure without narrowing future options?"

That is the systems test for taste. Not whether the player wins one level, but whether the engine is altering the player's field of perception in a way that improves the system's long-run behavior.

### ml-pragmatist

1. **Strongest idea**

The strongest idea is the LLM-as-player inversion.

Not because the metaphor is cute, but because it puts the trainable/non-trainable boundary in the right place. The model is mostly frozen. You are not improving its weights. You are controlling its observation stream, action space, reward proxies, and episode boundaries. That is an environment design problem.

In RL terms, "taste" is not primarily a learned internal policy here. It is the harness shaping the Markov blanket: what state is visible, what actions are available, what affordances are salient, and when the episode terminates. That is exactly where simple machinery can punch above its weight.

The camera/vantage language is useful if translated ruthlessly: camera position = state abstraction; lens = tool/interface; focus = loss/reward target; exposure = compute budget; framing = action-space restriction. This is not "prompt craft" in the fluffy sense. It is observation/action design for a very capable fixed policy.

That also explains why "immersion breaks" matter. A model flailing after receiving incoherent context is not a failure of intelligence. It is partial observability plus corrupted state. You handed the policy a bad game screen.

2. **What they got wrong or missed**

They are underweighting distribution shift and over-trusting synthetic practice.

Manufactured reps are attractive, but this field knows the trap: agents overfit to the simulator. If the practice arena is generated by the same system that consumes the lessons, you can get beautiful local improvement and zero transfer. Worse, you can train taste toward verifier quirks. The judgement machinery being "honest" is necessary, but not sufficient. It has to be independent enough from the generator and representative enough of real tasks.

The other miss: "n < 10" is probably fantasy unless the update target is very narrow. You can learn a rule, a rubric, or a routing heuristic from 2-9 failures. You do not learn broad taste. What you can do is maintain a small library of scars: "when this smell appears, try this move." That is valuable, but it is not the same object as expert discernment.

Also, the ledger risks becoming a hand-engineered ontology that feels explanatory while doing little. Seven primitives are fine if they become logged variables and interventions. If they stay conceptual, they are just names.

The hard question is not "what are the primitives?" It is: which primitive, when flipped, causally changes success rate?

3. **Concept missing**

They need **contextual bandits** on the table.

Not full RL. Full RL is too much machinery and too little signal. But taste-at-the-fork is almost exactly a contextual bandit: given task/context features, choose a framing/action among alternatives, observe a delayed/noisy reward from judgement.

This gives the project a clean 200-line version:

- Extract context features from the task and current run state.
- Generate 3-5 candidate framings: zoom levels, tools, personas, recon/build/search choices.
- Select with epsilon-greedy or Thompson sampling.
- Execute.
- Score with existing judgement.
- Update per-context action value estimates.

No gradients required. No giant learned policy. Just disciplined exploration. The important shift is that "taste" stops being mystical and becomes policy selection under uncertainty.

This also gives you regret as a north-star metric: how much cost was wasted by choosing a worse framing than another available framing?

4. **What I would do next**

I would build the dumb bandit harness before building any taste architecture.

First, reconstruct the camera variables from historical runs exactly as proposed, but only to produce features and candidate actions. Do not infer deep meaning yet.

Second, define 3-5 fork decisions that are common and consequential:

- decompose vs leap
- recon vs implement
- cheap search vs build lens/tool
- narrow context vs broad context
- solo worker vs adversarial/persona review

Third, create paired or triplet replays from real tasks. Same starting state, different framing, same judgement contract. This matters more than synthetic creativity. You need counterfactual comparisons.

Fourth, log every fork as:

```text
context_features, candidates, selected_action, selection_reason, cost, verifier_score, terminal_outcome
```

Fifth, implement the most boring policy possible: random exploration over candidate framings with a hand-written prior, then update action values by task cluster.

If this does not beat the current heuristic chooser after 50-100 fork decisions, the primitives are probably wrong or the reward is too noisy. If it does beat baseline, then you have a real taste loop: simple policy, real feedback, measurable regret reduction. That is the mechanism I would trust.
